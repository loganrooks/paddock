# Lane D: Support, Premium, Access, And Obligation

## Lane framing

`[governing:cited]` This lane closes the handoff-relative `monetization / premium posture` gap and most directly answers the emergent gap that support must be modeled as `support / access / service-obligation transitions`, not as a generic `free versus paid` choice (`05-gap-closure-context-and-plan.md:55-58`, `67-68`). It also answers the synthesis requirement that the closure bundle produce a positive doctrine-level statement about plausible support or premium posture while preserving the already-settled deferrals around paid guaranteed access and stronger public obligations (`05-gap-closure-synthesis.md:55-60`, `98-110`).

`[governing:cited]` This lane is not a blank monetization brainstorm. The handoff review already established that the remaining closure bundle must answer mature-product questions in archetype, shell, and obligation terms rather than as loose feature prompts (`05-handoff-gap-review.md:43-45`, `79-92`, `106-119`).

`[assumed:reasoned]` The goal here is therefore narrower and more useful: define which support and premium families still fit the repo's private-first doctrine, what paying should and should not buy, and where obligation meaningfully changes the product posture.

## What is already settled

`[evidenced:cited]` Several things are already canon and should constrain this lane rather than be rediscovered:

- `Support`, `access`, and `service obligation` are distinct concepts, and that distinction exists specifically to stop premature commercialization or public-platform assumptions from leaking into early architecture (`LONG-ARC.md:118-129`).
- `Trusted private access` is compatible with later visibility expansion; public discovery is not the default and should arrive only when the next obligation level is accepted deliberately (`LONG-ARC.md:81-102`; `PROJECT.md:144-146`).
- `Paid guaranteed access` and stronger public uptime or moderation promises remain deferred (`LONG-ARC.md:124-127`, `155-166`; `REQUIREMENTS.md:139-144`).
- `Wrapper access policy` and `visibility state` should remain separable from the shared content/session substrate, which means this lane cannot treat paid access as the master key that determines every future wrapper (`REQUIREMENTS.md:132-135`).
- The mature-product closure bundle needs a positive answer here, not only a deferral list. The closeout verifier explicitly said the mature-product/business/community layer remained only partially answered (`whole-job-verification-output.md:10-18`, `33-41`).

## Path of inquiry

`[evidenced/governing:mixed]` I anchored first in the gap-closure synthesis, handoff-review correction, and live canon to keep this lane doctrine-constrained. That established the non-negotiables: private-first center, staged visibility, low-friction browser access, explicit modest trust assumptions, and the existing support/access/service-obligation separation (`05-gap-closure-synthesis.md:43-69`; `PROJECT.md:138-146`; `LONG-ARC.md:81-129`).

`[assumed:reasoned]` Background-only but directly re-engaged here: the earlier repo-local research compared support models, selective-public access control, and trust burdens under widening publicness. That background did not decide the answer automatically, but it did sharpen which models create minimal promise debt versus which models immediately imply queue fairness, refunds, status communication, or moderation accountability (`02-funding-access-and-transparency-models.md:62-67`, `71-83`, `100-119`, `157-164`; `03-public-transition-and-discovery.md:86-115`; `04-security-trust-and-operational-risk.md:81-89`, `100-106`).

`[assumed:reasoned]` The core test was simple: which money-linked shapes preserve the repo's current product center, and which ones quietly turn Prix Guesser into a service that owes people dependable participation rather than a private-first authored game-night tool with some future seams preserved?

## Core closure questions

`[assumed:reasoned]` This lane needed to answer five questions:

1. What is the most coherent positive support posture before the project crosses into stronger public-service promises?
2. Which premium families should stay alive because they fit an authored, private-first, wrapper-based product?
3. Which premium families are weak fits because they mainly sell fairness advantages, publicness, or entitlement?
4. What access controls are acceptable as product-shape tools without becoming manipulative or silently donor-governed?
5. At what threshold does taking money change the obligation profile enough that the product posture itself has changed?

## Findings

### 1. The strongest early positive doctrine is `support-first, access-separate`

`[governing:cited]` The live canon already says optional support can exist without changing room access or service promises (`LONG-ARC.md:122-129`). The earlier funding/access research strongly reinforced that the cleanest early model for a small private-first hosted product is optional one-time or recurring support with no gameplay or access guarantee (`02-funding-access-and-transparency-models.md:62-63`, `71-77`, `157-160`).

`[assumed:reasoned]` This lane therefore closes one important ambiguity positively: Prix Guesser does not need to remain `money-free` in order to remain doctrinally coherent. What it needs is a support posture that does not quietly convert goodwill into entitlement.

`[assumed:reasoned]` Concretely, the healthiest earliest rung is:

- optional one-time or recurring support
- no gameplay advantage
- no fairness advantage in live rooms
- no silent guarantee of access
- plain language about what support funds: continuity, infra cost, and authored work

### 2. `Access` should be governed by surface and capacity, not by donor status

`[governing:cited]` The project already distinguishes trusted private rooms, share-by-link or bounded surfaces, and later selectively visible shells (`LONG-ARC.md:91-102`; `PROJECT.md:144-146`). The public-transition research adds the useful comparative rule that `room access`, `challenge access`, `spectator access`, and `public interest capture` are separate surfaces with different trust burdens (`03-public-transition-and-discovery.md:86-99`, `107-115`, `157-159`).

`[assumed:reasoned]` That means access control can be real without being monetized. Invite-only betas, waitlists, capacity windows, and public read surfaces can all be legitimate if they describe actual operator capacity or actual wrapper boundaries. They become corrosive when they are driven by supporter privilege or scarcity theater instead of real operational constraints.

`[assumed:reasoned]` Background-only but directly re-engaged here: the earlier funding lane is especially clear on this point. Honest capacity windows and queues can be ethically tolerable, but payment-linked queue skipping or vague scarcity language are among the highest-risk trust traps for a small evolving service (`02-funding-access-and-transparency-models.md:123-141`, `147-153`, `161-164`).

### 3. The premium families worth preserving are `official hosted convenience` and `editorial programming/content`

`[governing:cited]` The synthesis explicitly asked this lane to keep multiple plausible monetization families alive, especially optional support, hosted convenience, premium programming/content, and supporter/community-host contribution paths, without forcing exact pricing or packaging (`05-gap-closure-synthesis.md:55-67`, `106-110`).

`[assumed:reasoned]` The best-fit premium families are not all equally strong.

`[assumed:reasoned]` The two families that deserve active carry-forward are:

- `Official hosted convenience`
  What it means: paying later for an easier, more managed, more reliable official hosted path than self-operator flow, not for gameplay power or privileged truth surfaces.
  Why it fits: it maps to the repo's hosting ladder and browser-first guest doctrine without requiring public discovery or a second product core (`LONG-ARC.md:104-114`, `145-149`).

- `Editorial programming/content`
  What it means: curated authored packs, premium event programming, supporter-visible previews, or later editorial drops that sell curation and authorship rather than social privilege.
  Why it fits: this is an authored product with a strong curation center, so premium content/programming is more coherent than premium competitive status. The exact content cadence remains Lane A territory, but the family itself should stay alive here (`PROJECT.md:140-146`; `05-gap-closure-synthesis.md:58-60`).

`[assumed:reasoned]` By contrast, these premium families are weak fits and should not be treated as preferred carry-forward:

- `premium fairness` such as queue priority, guaranteed room slots, or larger participation rights before the service is mature
- `premium publicness` such as buying into stranger-facing rooms or public discovery
- `premium social privilege` such as moderation exemptions or special participant authority

Those families fit mature public services better than a private-first authored game-night product.

### 4. The actual posture change happens when money buys dependable participation

`[governing:cited]` The canon already defers paid guaranteed access and strong uptime promises (`LONG-ARC.md:126-129`, `155-166`; `PROJECT.md:146`). The earlier funding research sharpened the practical threshold: once payment buys guaranteed access, reservation rights, or materially better service, the project has taken on a real support, refund, and reliability burden (`02-funding-access-and-transparency-models.md:64-66`, `100-106`, `157-164`).

`[assumed:reasoned]` Background-only but directly re-engaged here: the earlier trust/risk research makes the same point from the operational side. Publicness raises obligation in steps, and unclear queueing, downtime, or room-status ambiguity becomes reputational debt before it becomes a purely technical problem (`04-security-trust-and-operational-risk.md:81-89`, `100-106`, `133-140`).

`[assumed:reasoned]` This lane therefore recommends a clear threshold rule:

- `Taking money` is not by itself the posture change.
- `Taking money in exchange for dependable participation` is the posture change.

`[assumed:reasoned]` In practice, the threshold is crossed when one or more of these become true:

- payment buys guaranteed access to an official hosted surface
- payment buys queue priority, reservations, or materially better admission rights
- payment implies incident-response, refund, or support-handling expectations if the service is down
- payment buys a public-facing promise of continuity rather than a best-effort hobby posture

At that point the product is no longer merely accepting support. It is acting like a service with customers.

### 5. `Community-host contribution` is related, but it is not the same ladder

`[governing:cited]` The synthesis deliberately separated Lane D from the grassroots transition-hosting lane and explicitly rejected merging them (`05-gap-closure-synthesis.md:61-67`, `114-120`). That split is correct.

`[assumed:reasoned]` Background-only but directly re-engaged here: the earlier funding work also found that money and infrastructure are not interchangeable. Monetary support mostly preserves one canonical hosted experience; community hosting changes topology, governance, privacy, and support ownership (`02-funding-access-and-transparency-models.md:110-119`, `185-191`).

`[assumed:reasoned]` This lane therefore carries forward only one boundary judgment:

- `supporter/community-host contribution paths` should stay alive as a comparison family
- but `who can host and under what trust boundary` remains Lane E's primary closure question

Lane D should not pretend that community-host contribution is just another premium perk.

### 6. What paying should and should not buy

`[assumed:reasoned]` If the project ever charges for anything, the coherent buys are:

- voluntary support and recognition
- light supporter perks that do not alter room fairness
- later official hosted convenience
- later editorial programming/content

`[assumed:reasoned]` It should not buy:

- answer power, scoring advantage, or gameplay leverage
- queue-skipping or silent priority in live-room fairness surfaces
- stranger-room or public-discovery privilege
- moderation exemptions or informal host authority
- implied perpetual uptime or incident-response guarantees unless the project deliberately accepts that service posture

## Live options or operating shapes

`[assumed:reasoned]` The live comparison that best fits current doctrine is:

| Operating shape | What money is for | Access effect | Obligation profile | Carry-forward judgment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `Optional support only` | continuity, hosting cost, authored work | none | low promise debt | strongest early rung |
| `Light supporter tiers` | recognition, notes, previews, occasional light perks | none or near-none | low-medium; recurring fulfillment burden | still alive if perks stay concrete and non-entitling |
| `Bounded beta or waitlist plus optional support` | support stays optional; access is governed separately by capacity or learning goals | real access control, but not donor-governed | medium; requires honest status/capacity language | good transition shape |
| `Official hosted convenience` | easier managed remote access or official-host friction reduction | may affect where people play, but should not imply fairness privilege by default | medium-high; becomes a service surface quickly | keep alive as a later premium family |
| `Editorial programming/content` | curated premium packs, event series, previews, or authored drops | affects content/program access, not room fairness | medium; depends on content operations maturity more than infra | keep alive as a later premium family |
| `Paid guaranteed access / priority` | dependable participation, reserved slots, materially better service | yes, directly | high; requires status, incident, and support-accountability posture | explicitly deferred for now |

`[assumed:reasoned]` The main comparative conclusion is that Prix Guesser should think in terms of a `ladder`, not one monetization thesis:

- support can arrive before premium
- access widening can happen separately from support
- convenience and editorial programming are stronger premium fits than fairness or publicness
- guaranteed access is the point where a different obligation profile begins

## Recommended carry-forward

`[projected:reasoned]` Recommended doctrine to carry into mature-product synthesis:

1. `Support-first posture`
   Optional support is the default positive answer for the near-term mature-product story. It is compatible with the private-first center and does not require a public-service identity.

2. `Access remains a separate axis`
   Invite windows, waitlists, challenge links, spectator visibility, and live-room participation should remain surface-specific decisions. They should not be silently turned into supporter privileges.

3. `Keep two premium families alive`
   Preserve `official hosted convenience` and `editorial programming/content` as the strongest later premium families.

4. `Treat guaranteed access as a posture change`
   The moment money buys dependable participation, the project has crossed into a stronger service obligation and must say so plainly.

5. `Do not merge contribution topology into the premium ladder`
   Supporter-funded or community-assisted hosting may matter later, but the topology and trust-boundary answer still belongs to Lane E.

`[projected:reasoned]` Downstream consequence for mature-product synthesis:

- the synthesis should present one `support -> access-control -> later premium family -> obligation threshold` ladder
- it should not collapse the answer into a single subscription thesis or a single host-topology thesis

`[projected:reasoned]` Downstream consequence for the later sensitivity pass:

- test whether any proposed wording quietly turns support into entitlement
- test whether any premium family implies public-service promises earlier than intended
- test whether access controls remain tied to real surface/capacity constraints rather than FOMO or donor privilege

`[projected:reasoned]` Downstream consequence for canon patching, if later confirmed:

- `LONG-ARC.md` and `PROJECT.md` likely need a more positive ladder statement, not only negative deferrals
- `REQUIREMENTS.md` should probably keep support/access/obligation distinctions as seams or deferrals, not as active commercialization requirements
- `ROADMAP.md` should avoid language that implies Milestone 1 or default Milestone 2 already includes paid hosted guarantees

`[projected:reasoned]` The later sensitivity pass should test:

- whether any carry-forward wording accidentally implies supporter priority, guaranteed official hosting, or refund-like expectation before the project intends that shift

- whether `editorial programming/content` can stay alive without forcing Lane A's content cadence answer prematurely

## Coverage note

`[coverage:reasoned]` This lane closes the doctrine-level answer for support, premium, access, and obligation. The positive answer is: `optional support` is the strongest early rung; `access` should remain surface- and capacity-governed rather than donor-governed; `official hosted convenience` and `editorial programming/content` are the premium families most worth preserving; and `paid guaranteed access` remains the threshold that would change the product into a stronger service posture.

`[coverage:reasoned]` What remains open is not silence-by-accident. Exact pricing, exact packaging, detailed host-topology choices, and exact editorial premium shape remain intentionally deferred to later synthesis or adjacent lanes. That means this lane is sufficient for mature-product synthesis input, but not by itself sufficient for canon patching until it is reconciled with Lanes A, C, and E plus the later sensitivity pass.
