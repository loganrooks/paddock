# Round 2A Lane B Output

## Lane framing

`[governing:cited]` Lane B exists to map the experience class where Prix Guesser is synchronous but not co-present: private online groups, Discord-group play, hidden-info remote play, and separated-local forms that behave like remote topology because secrecy, trust, and private views matter more than shared-stage spectacle. This lane directly supports `RESP-03`, most strongly informs `GAP-03`, `GAP-04`, and `GAP-07`, contributes evidence toward `RESP-04`, and does not itself close `RESP-04` (`03-next-round/round-2a-lane-b-private-online-task-spec.md:23-49`).

`[assumed:reasoned]` The central correction is to treat remote sync as its own social medium rather than as degraded couch play. The interesting question here is not "how do we preserve local party energy at distance?" but "what becomes newly strong once each player can have a private view, voice becomes the main social channel, and the room must tolerate uneven presence?" This reading follows the lane calibration and the handoff's Thread 2 split between local, online synchronous, online asynchronous, and hybrid experiences (`03-next-round/round-2a-lane-b-private-online-task-spec.md:51-81`; `HANDOFF.md:96-104`).

## Calibration carry-forward

`[governing:cited]` This lane preserves four local corrections from its task spec:

- remote play is not just lesser couch play
- examples are probes rather than taxonomies
- asymmetric or topology-sensitive forms should not be flattened into generic online categories
- lived interaction shape matters more than feature buckets

These are explicit lane instructions, not optional tone notes (`03-next-round/round-2a-lane-b-private-online-task-spec.md:51-58`).

`[governing:cited]` The wider Round 2A calibration also stays in force here: promising ideas are design terrain, one ontology should not be imposed across the portfolio, cultural charge and mechanical spine may both matter, and the round should not smuggle large-room feasibility back into the same lane as broad experience exposure (`03-next-round/round-2a-experience-archetypes-task-spec.md:63-75`, `03-next-round/round-2a-experience-archetypes-task-spec.md:143-151`).

`[assumed:reasoned]` I therefore treat "private Discord room with voice and private screens," "same house but split across devices or rooms," and "host-guided hidden-info co-op" as probes of the same topology family. They are not declared to be one final taxonomy. They are ways to see what this lane notices.

## What this experience feels like in practice

`[assumed:reasoned]` The strongest version of this experience is a trusted small group arriving in a voice room with individual screens and a shared expectation that talk itself is part of the game. The energy is less "watch the room erupt together at the TV" and more "hear each person's certainty crack, bluff, over-explain, stall, or reveal too much." Remote sync strips away ambient body language and shared physical spectacle, but it increases the importance of timing in speech, confidence signaling, selective disclosure, and private interpretation.

`[assumed:reasoned]` Three probes seem especially revealing:

- `voice-room deduction`: everyone can talk, but not everyone sees the same clues or answer state. Suspicion, confidence, and clarification become the social fuel.
- `asymmetric remote co-op`: one player or a rotating role sees privileged information and must steer without collapsing the puzzle. This turns trust and role discipline into the main drama.
- `separated-local/private-device play`: players may be in the same home or venue, but if they use personal screens, partial separation, or hidden packets, the interaction pressures resemble remote sync more than classic couch play.

`[evidenced:cited]` This reading aligns with the handoff's insistence that online synchronous and hybrid play create fundamentally different social textures from local party play (`HANDOFF.md:98-104`). It also sharpens Round 1's note that some modes are "remote specialists" rather than universal fits (`01-round-1/round-1-output.md:207-216`).

`[assumed:reasoned]` What survives remote sync well is discussion, suspicion, secret submission, confidence play, and role-framed cooperation. What needs compensation is shared spectacle, instant room laughter, embodied chaos, and glance-based reading of others. The compensation is not necessarily more UI. Often it is better cadence, stronger reveal staging, and more deliberate use of private information.

## What kinds of game territories fit or resist it

`[evidenced:cited]` Round 1 already hinted that `The Grid Walk` may be one of the strongest "remote sync / discussion-first co-op" candidates in the corpus, and that Lane B really contains distinct pleasures including debate theater, strategy hindsight, and asymmetric information co-op (`01-round-1/round-1-output.md:119-123`, `01-round-1/round-1-output.md:155-163`). Lane B makes that split more valuable, not less.

`[assumed:reasoned]` The game territories that fit this experience especially well are:

- discussion-first co-op where information is incomplete or unevenly distributed
- judgment and verdict formats where secret votes, confidence statements, or private evidence packets sharpen the reveal
- hidden-submission reveal games where private entries are authored or improvised, then surfaced in a staged shared reveal
- role asymmetry modes where one player guides, withholds, or frames rather than everyone doing the same action simultaneously

`[assumed:reasoned]` Under that lens, several existing territories read better:

- `The Grid Walk` rises because remote sync naturally supports conversational clue exchange and collaborative narrowing.
- `The Stewards' Room` becomes stronger when the room can privately commit verdicts, compare confidence, or argue from asymmetric briefs rather than merely react together.
- `Team Principal's Desk` improves if it stops being a thin symmetric "pit / stay out" shell and becomes remote team-talk, asymmetric counsel, or confidence calibration, which Round 1 already suspected (`01-round-1/round-1-output.md:109-117`).
- `Words of Wisdom`, `Meme Prompts`, and some `Paddock Fashion` branches can survive remote sync if submission is private and reveal is shared; they weaken if they depend mostly on co-present laughter before the reveal (`01-round-1/round-1-output.md:42-51`, `01-round-1/round-1-output.md:65-76`, `01-round-1/round-1-output.md:125-133`).

`[assumed:reasoned]` The territories that resist this lane are the ones whose core pleasure is shared-stage spectacle, embodied chaos, or instant mutual legibility. Pure host-screen theater, action-heavy predator-hunter play, and physically legible coordination spikes lose their natural advantage here. `Pit Stop Co-op` may still work as a short coordination spike, but its ceiling likely drops because it trades on immediacy more than private interpretation (`01-round-1/round-1-output.md:135-139`, `01-round-1/round-1-output.md:165-170`).

## What this experience wants from topology, authority, visibility, and cadence

`[assumed:reasoned]` This experience wants topology to begin from personal screens, not from a single authoritative shared surface. A shared reveal surface may still help, but it is optional garnish unless the reveal itself needs collective staging. The more important primitive is mixed visibility: some information is room-visible, some is role-visible, some is privately held until reveal.

`[assumed:reasoned]` It wants authority to be explicit. Hidden information cannot depend on honor alone forever if the product wants to support recurring play with low friction. The system or host needs to control who sees what, when submissions lock, when reveals fire, and when role-specific instructions appear. That is not because private groups are untrustworthy. It is because trust should power the social layer, not carry the bookkeeping burden.

`[assumed:reasoned]` It wants cadence that leaves room for speech. Remote sync is usually stronger when it alternates between discuss, commit, and reveal rather than forcing everyone into twitch simultaneity. A few extra seconds of intentional silence or lock-in is often socially productive here, because voice-room play needs a clear phase boundary.

`[assumed:reasoned]` It also wants resilience to imperfect presence: muted cameras, laggy microphones, someone briefly dropping and rejoining, one player participating by phone audio while another is fully on-screen. These are not edge cases for this lane. They are part of the lived topology. Separated-local forms inherit many of the same needs.

## What this experience does to privacy, recurrence, and community

`[assumed:reasoned]` Privacy in this lane is not just safety or account security. It is a design material. Secret guesses, private clue packets, confidential roles, and staged reveal timing are part of what makes remote sync feel distinct. The product should therefore think of privacy as both experiential and social.

`[assumed:reasoned]` Recurrence here is more likely to be room-based than feed-based. The most plausible repeat loop is not "the public comes back every day to a universal challenge." It is "our Discord group runs this on race weekends," "our friend group has recurring private packs," or "we keep a room history because the same people return." This fits the handoff's question about social experiences beyond one session and the gap review's concern with under-synthesized mature-product/community layers (`HANDOFF.md:103-114`; `00-governance/next-round-gap-review.md:376-390`).

`[assumed:reasoned]` Community therefore looks intimate before it looks public. Group histories, recurring in-jokes, shared packet libraries, seasonal mini-leagues among friends, and memory of prior betrayals or brilliant reads are more central than broad discovery features. This also matches Round 1's judgment that expression-heavy modes tend to read best as private-first before curated public/community extension (`01-round-1/round-1-output.md:172-179`).

`[assumed:reasoned]` Mature-product value in this lane may come less from mass audience scale and more from making small-group recurrence feel worth organizing: better private-room ritual, replayable hidden-info formats, good reveal staging, and persistent shared history. Private-room trust and voice dynamics matter more here than raw networking bravado.

## What this lane changes about existing game judgments

`[assumed:reasoned]` This lane upgrades remote-specialist reads. `The Grid Walk` is not merely an odd side branch; it looks like one of the most archetypal fits for synchronous-not-co-present play. `The Stewards' Room` also gains weight because remote voice channels can intensify orderly argument and secret commitment rather than dilute them.

`[assumed:reasoned]` It changes how `Team Principal's Desk` should be judged. Its problem is less "can we make the symmetric version deeper?" and more "should this territory be re-authored around remote role asymmetry, privileged briefs, and confidence communication?" That pushes it toward a much more native lane fit than Round 1's thinner shell suggested.

`[assumed:reasoned]` It softens but does not erase the fit of expressive reveal modes. `Words of Wisdom`, `Meme Prompts`, and some `Paddock Fashion` descendants still have a place here, but mostly when private creation and staged reveal are load-bearing. If their core pleasure depends on co-present collective laughter before or during creation, their best home is elsewhere.

`[assumed:reasoned]` It makes action-chaos branches look more conditional. A remote version may still exist, but it would need either unusually strong netcode or a redesign around planning, prediction, or role-split coordination. Lane B therefore reinforces Round 1's broader claim that not every mode needs every context (`01-round-1/round-1-output.md:183-191`).

## What remains uncertain

`[open:reasoned]` It is still unclear how much of this lane needs voice and how much can survive in text chat plus strong reveal pacing. Voice seems central to the current read, but the audit has not tested whether text-first remote groups could produce a similarly rich social texture.

`[open:reasoned]` It is still unclear how much hidden-state complexity private groups will tolerate before the game starts to feel like admin work. The lane strongly suggests mixed visibility matters, but not yet what the practical limit is.

`[open:reasoned]` It is still unclear how far the anchor geography/circuit fantasy can stretch in this lane. Private maps, secret location briefs, and collaborative narrowing all seem plausible, but remote sync may privilege talk-heavy inference more than pure place-recognition spectacle.

`[open:reasoned]` It is still unclear whether separated-local and fully remote should be treated as one implementation family or as siblings with materially different reveal needs. They share topology pressures, but may not share identical cadence.

`[open:reasoned]` This lane does not resolve how much anti-cheat or verification pressure a mature private-online product actually needs. Trust matters here, but so do low-friction guardrails.

## What to carry into foreclosure synthesis

`[projected:reasoned]` The later foreclosure synthesis should test whether early architecture keeps the following open:

- per-player private state as a first-class primitive
- mixed-visibility rounds rather than one universally shared state
- role-specific instructions and asymmetry
- deliberate phase transitions between discussion, commit, and reveal
- room models that tolerate partial presence, reconnection, and separated-local participation
- persistent private-room history, recurrence, and group memory

`[projected:reasoned]` It should specifically avoid assuming that the mature product is centered on one shared host screen, one common information surface, or one submission grammar. Those assumptions would privilege Lane A and silently weaken this lane before the comparison is finished.

`[projected:reasoned]` It should also treat topology-sensitive local forms as part of the same future-facing pressure family as remote sync, because private devices, partial separation, and asymmetric visibility often matter more than physical distance itself.

`[projected:reasoned]` This lane still does not decide transport, room-authority implementation, or exact runtime architecture. It only sharpens which experiential capacities the later architecture synthesis must preserve.

## Framework Invisibility

`[open:reasoned]` No matter how rigorously this lane is conducted, it will under-see the pleasures that depend on co-present spectacle, public audience dynamics, or solo ritual return loops. A lane framed around private online sync is structurally better at noticing trust, secrecy, role asymmetry, and voice-room cadence than it is at noticing the value of one glowing shared reveal screen or the retention logic of daily solo return.

## What the Obligations Didn't Capture

`[assumed:reasoned]` The strongest extra observation is that scheduling burden is part of the experience here. Remote sync is not just a topology problem. It is also a social-coordination problem. A mode can be excellent in this lane and still underperform if it asks the group to organize too much effort too often. That pressure does not fit neatly inside topology, privacy, or community, but it materially shapes what kinds of remote experiences will actually recur.

## Rule 5: Frame-Reflexivity

`[open:reasoned]` If this had been classified under a local-party subject instead, it would have looked much harder at host timing, shared-stage spectacle, and the collective gasp/laugh dynamics that this lane intentionally routes away from Lane B (`03-next-round/round-2a-lane-b-private-online-task-spec.md:46-49`). If it had been framed as standard `architecture-foreclosure` work, it would have closed sooner on technical primitives and hidden the fact that the experience question is still open. One concrete finding this lane cannot surface on its own is whether Prix Guesser's mature center of gravity should remain a shared-screen spectacle product with remote support as a stretch. This frame is designed to notice where remote/private topology is first-class; it cannot answer whether remote/private topology should dominate the product.
