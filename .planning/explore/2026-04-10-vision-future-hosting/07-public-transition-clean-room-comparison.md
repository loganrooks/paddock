## High-Level Result

The clean-room rerun did not overturn the earlier lane.

It largely confirmed the earlier result that:

- public transition should be understood as layered surfaces rather than a single private/public switch
- live-room participation should stay private longer than some other surfaces
- async challenges and spectator or streamer surfaces are the strongest candidates for earlier semi-public visibility
- discovery doctrine depends on wrapper choice, not generic growth advice

But it did contribute a meaningful reframing.

The original lane was stronger on:

- surface taxonomy
- detailed feasibility-by-surface table
- explicit treatment of moderation and identity burden
- richer treatment of playtest, waitlist, and public-discovery precedents

The clean-room rerun was stronger on:

- asking which surfaces can become visible without collapsing the product's intimacy and operator-manageability
- explicitly framing public transition as a `visibility state` problem, not just a discovery problem
- surfacing the possibility that a sibling public-facing challenge or creator surface might split from the private room product earlier than the roadmap language currently implies

## What Stayed Stable

- the current product soul still looks live, host-led, curated, and private-first
- the safest early publicness still appears to be surface-specific rather than global
- live rooms still look like the worst place to open up first
- async challenge sharing still looks like the cleanest first semi-public experiment
- spectator or streamer visibility still looks like a plausible earlier public edge than open live-room participation

## What The Clean-Room Rerun Added

### 1. Visibility State Became More Central Than Discovery Tactic

The original lane emphasized `surface layering`.

The clean-room rerun kept that, but made the deeper issue more explicit:

- the real design question is which surfaces can become visible or shareable without changing what the product is

That is a stronger framing for this project because it keeps the product-identity question in view instead of slipping into growth-language too early.

### 2. The Product-Family Split Became More Thinkable

The clean-room rerun introduced a live possibility that the project may eventually need:

- a mostly private room product
- and a sibling public challenge or creator-facing surface

The original lane hinted at wrapper differentiation, but the clean-room rerun made the possible family split more explicit.

This matters because it connects directly back to the earlier product-futures rerun distinction between:

- wrapper
- stage-shape
- sibling product

### 3. Curated Feature Loops Became More Legible

The clean-room rerun was clearer about a `curated feature loop`:

- featured packs
- featured challenges
- showcase events

as a possible public-discovery path that is neither:

- open marketplace logic
- nor pure friend-network invisibility

That gives the exploration a more concrete middle layer to think with.

### 4. Public Transition Was Framed More Explicitly As A Service-Obligation Problem

The original lane already handled trust burdens well.

The clean-room rerun sharpened the point that once a surface becomes public enough, the product acquires:

- stronger explanation obligations
- stronger revocation and reporting expectations
- more durable visibility-state semantics

This complements the hosting and security lanes by making publicness feel like a governance threshold, not merely a traffic threshold.

## What The Earlier High Lane Still Did Better

### 1. Better Surface Taxonomy

The earlier lane gave a more explicit breakdown of:

- public landing
- waitlist
- async challenge
- live room
- spectator
- pack authorship

and tied that to a feasibility table.

The clean-room rerun was conceptually strong, but somewhat less systematic in that matrix form.

### 2. Better Moderation / Identity Integration

The earlier lane was stronger on:

- no-account join versus moderation tension
- Twitch and Jackbox moderation precedents
- publicness as a ratcheting trust burden

The clean-room rerun still saw that burden, but it foregrounded visibility-state and product-shape questions more than moderation architecture.

### 3. Better Explicit Experimental Sequencing

The earlier lane was more direct about likely first experiments:

- invited host cohort
- bounded async challenge sharing
- read-only waitlist interest capture

The clean-room rerun still points in that direction, but with less operational sequencing detail.

## Net Interpretation

The clean-room rerun did produce a useful reframing.

It did not mainly tell us:

- "the old lane was wrong"

It told us:

- the old lane was broadly right
- but public transition should be thought about even more explicitly as a `visibility-state and product-boundary` problem
- and the possibility of a sibling public-facing surface should stay alive rather than being silently forced into one app-level trajectory

That is a meaningful result.

It reinforces the broader exploration trend:

- the later and cleaner reruns are most useful when they make the project step back and ask whether one neat product trajectory is hiding a family of related but not identical futures

## Practical Implication For Discussion

When we return to the vision discussion, the strongest new pressure from this clean-room rerun is:

- we should not only ask which wrapper becomes public first
- we should also ask whether the first public-facing surface is actually part of the same product shell as the private room ritual, or whether it begins to act more like a sibling challenge or showcase product

That is probably the most discussion-worthy addition from this rerun.
